## Where I learned to debug

This is the oldest thing on my CV and it is the reason I am any good at debugging.

Software on a robot cannot lie to you comfortably. There is no console to read once it is on the
field — there is a machine physically doing the wrong thing while a match clock runs. Sensors return
noisy values, the same code behaves differently on carpet than on tile, and a fault can be
mechanical, electrical or yours. You learn very quickly to form a specific hypothesis and test it,
rather than changing something plausible and hoping.

That habit is exactly what I used years later at ANTSA to find a bug that produced no error and no
log entry, just quietly wrong data. Same discipline: stop trusting the layer that looks fine, and
follow the actual values.

## The other half

I was also the person explaining our software decisions to mentors. Presenting technical progress to
someone who could ask a hard question at any point is a genuinely good forcing function — it is very
hard to hand-wave through an explanation of something you do not actually understand.
